How phishing actually works

Phishing is a fake message made to look like it came from someone you trust, so that you’ll click, log in or pay without thinking. It doesn’t work because people are careless. It works because the message is built to make you rush.

The usual disguises

A bank saying your “KYC is expiring” or your account will be blocked. A courier asking for a small fee to deliver a parcel. A job offer that needs a registration payment. A friend’s hacked account sending a link. Every one of them pushes the same three buttons: fear, urgency or reward.

How to read a link

The real website is the part just before the first single slash. Take sbi.co.in.verify-login.com/update. The site you’d actually land on is verify-login.com. The bank’s name at the front is only decoration.

What to do

  • Don’t click. Open the app or type the website address yourself.
  • No genuine bank asks for your OTP, PIN or CVV, ever.
  • Pause on anything urgent. A real problem will still be there in ten minutes.
  • If you’ve been scammed in India, call 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in as quickly as you can, since speed matters for stopping a payment.

Passwords: length beats cleverness

Most passwords fail for two reasons: they’re easy to guess, or they’re used on more than one site. Fixing both matters more than adding a symbol.

Why “Password@123” fails

When a site forces a capital, a number and a symbol, people satisfy the rules in the laziest way: capital first, number last, @ for a. Attackers know this, and their tools try those patterns first.

Length wins

NIST, the US standards body whose password guidance is widely followed, now recommends allowing long passwords and not forcing composition rules or regular changes. Four random words, like purple-tiffin-rainy-bus, are easy to remember and far harder to guess than a short “complex” one.

Reuse is the real danger

When one site is breached, attackers try the same email and password on other sites. This is called credential stuffing. If every site has its own password, one breach stays one breach.

  • Use a password manager so you only have to remember one strong password.
  • Make your email password the strongest and most unique one, since it resets everything else.
  • Check whether your email appeared in a known breach at haveibeenpwned.com.

Public Wi-Fi: how risky is it really?

Public Wi-Fi has a scary reputation. The truth is more boring: it’s safer than it used to be, but there are still a few real traps.

What’s better than before

Most websites now use HTTPS, which means what you send is encrypted between your phone and the site, even on a café network. The people running the network can usually see which sites you visit, but not the contents of a properly secured page.

What can still go wrong

A fake hotspot with a name close to the real one is the classic trap, sometimes called an evil twin. Others are login pages that ask for far more personal detail than they need, security warnings people click past, and phones that haven’t been updated.

What about a VPN?

A VPN hides your traffic from the local network, but it moves your trust to the VPN company instead. It helps in some situations. It isn’t magic.

  • Ask staff for the exact network name before connecting.
  • Never click through a certificate or security warning.
  • For banking, use your mobile data if you’re unsure.
  • Turn off auto-connect to open networks and keep your phone updated.

Two-factor authentication: what it stops, and what it doesn’t

Two-factor authentication (2FA) means that a password alone isn’t enough. You also need a second proof, usually something you have, like your phone.

The common kinds

SMS codes are better than nothing, but they can be intercepted through a SIM swap or tricked out of you. Authenticator apps generate codes on your phone without sending them over the network, which is stronger. Security keys and passkeys are the strongest, because they only work on the genuine website, so a fake page can’t collect them.

What 2FA doesn’t stop

If a scammer calls pretending to be your bank and you read out the OTP, 2FA hasn’t failed. You’ve handed over the second lock yourself. Some fake login pages can also pass your code to the real site in real time.

  • Turn it on for your email first, since email can reset your other accounts.
  • Prefer an authenticator app over SMS when a site offers both.
  • Never share an OTP with anyone, even someone who sounds official.
  • Save the backup codes somewhere safe in case you lose your phone.

What app permissions really mean

Every permission is a door into your phone. Plenty of apps ask for more doors than they need, and most people tap “Allow” without reading.

The ones worth a second look

Location, microphone, camera, contacts, and photos or files. The classic warning sign is a simple torch or wallpaper app that asks for your contacts. Ask what the app would need that for.

“While using” beats “always”

Most apps only need access while you’re using them. Choose while using the app or ask every time, and save always for things like navigation or fitness tracking that genuinely run in the background.

Do a two-minute audit

Open your phone’s settings and look for Privacy or App permissions (the exact name varies by phone). Go through each permission and see which apps have it. Remove anything you don’t recognise or don’t use.

  • Install apps from the official store only.
  • Delete apps you no longer use, since each one is another door.
  • Read what an app asks for the first time it asks, not after.
